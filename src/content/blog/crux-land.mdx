## What I learned about buying land in the US

I came into CRUX-Land knowing approximately nothing about how rural land actually trades. What I do know now is mostly what the Designer surfaced in the protocol it generated from my one-sentence task brief — five categories of failure mode I hadn't thought of going in, encoded as criteria the agent would check against each parcel before pursuing it. Walking them.

### Where in the US does the parcel sit?

I'd assumed "real estate" was a single nationwide thing. It isn't — the legal substrate varies state by state. **Louisiana** is the only civil-law state in the country, with conveyances running on a Napoleonic Code substrate rather than the common-law title-chain machinery that works in the other 49; title insurance works differently, and the parish-recorder system isn't quite the county-recorder system. **New York** requires attorney representation at residential closings, which puts a hard fee floor of $1,500–2,000 on every transaction. The protocol excludes both (46 states in scope), not because cheap rural land isn't available in them but because the legal-substrate divergence costs more to handle than the parcels would. Counties differ too: some run e-recording (Simplifile and CSC eRecording are the dominant SaaS providers) with 1–5 business day turnaround, while others still require physical mail and turn documents around on a 4–12 week cadence. A parcel in a six-month-recording county is incompatible with a three-week experiment.

### Is it actually buildable?

This was the most counterintuitive thing I picked up — a lot of "rural land" can't legally have a structure on it, and the failure modes don't show up in listing photos. Forest-resource zoning (Washington State's "F" zones, Oregon's EFU and Forest split) explicitly prohibits residential construction even on a 100-acre parcel; the land is reserved for active forestry. Landlocked parcels have no recorded right-of-way to a public road, which means you can't legally drive or walk to them without trespassing on a neighbor's land — these are common in the cheap-rural channel because parent parcels got subdivided across generations without easements being preserved, and you can buy one for $300 at a tax-deed auction but you can't build on it, access it, or sell it without a $3,000–15,000 quiet-title action. FEMA Special Flood Hazard Areas (Zone A, AE, V) trigger mandatory NFIP coverage at $800–3,000/year regardless of structure type. Minimum-lot-size rules vary 0.25–2 acres by county, with one acre as the conventional safe floor — but I learned the rule has two interesting failure modes: parcels larger than an acre that are still unbuildable (the F-zone case), and parcels smaller than an acre that are buildable as legal nonconforming lots under **lot-of-record exceptions**. CRUX-Land's protocol started with a ≥1-acre rule, I relaxed it on Day 1, and that relaxation later let Siskiyou's 0.22-acre wedge through — the central story of Part 3.

### Even if buildable, can you actually live off-grid on it?

I had to learn this twice. The first time was through HOA covenants. Many cheap rural subdivisions — Costilla CO is the canonical example, and was the first channel we evaluated — were marketed during the 1960s–70s back-to-the-land boom as homestead opportunities, then in the 1980s and 90s the HOA boards added covenants that effectively banned the homestead use case: minimum-square-footage requirements, no mobile homes, mandatory septic permits and inspections, no rainwater catchment, no composting toilets. These covenants are recorded with the county as CC&Rs (Covenants, Conditions & Restrictions), but you have to pull and read them yourself. The second time was through county code enforcement. Modoc CA's Terms of Sale paragraph 11 prohibits septic holding tanks, water hauling, composting toilets, incinerator toilets, and rainwater collection as a potable source — with criminal enforcement on camping. The Modoc lots got killed on this exact paragraph, and the protocol added a code-enforcement-posture criterion post-run (covered in Part 3).

### What market are you actually shopping in?

I'd thought of the $1,500 cap as an arbitrary budget choice. It isn't — it's a channel selector. Sub-$1,500 raw land doesn't trade on MLS; it moves through **county tax-deed sales**, where parcels that defaulted on property tax get seized by the county and auctioned to recoup the unpaid bill. The seller is the county itself rather than a private party, and the transaction comes with no realtor, no listing agent, no inspection contingency, and no home warranty. The buyer's diligence floor is whatever the buyer brings — exactly the kind of market where an agent that can read 200-page county PDFs has an advantage over a human who can't. Two adjacent signals turned out to be useful for telling the right channel from the wrong one. Annual property tax is one: a high tax bill signals the parcel sits inside a municipal-services zone (electricity, water, sewer, road maintenance, fire protection), which is the opposite of what off-grid wants — CRUX-Land's eventual Siskiyou parcel had $11.52/year. Listing channel is the other: if the parcel is on Zillow or Realtor.com at sub-$1,500, something is wrong with it.

### Anti-fraud floors at closing

I didn't realize this going in, but the cheap-rural-parcel demographic attracts fake sellers, forged deeds, and dual-conveyance schemes. The protocol layers two anti-fraud floors. Title commitment from a licensed title insurer (one of the four national underwriters — Fidelity, First American, Stewart, Old Republic — or a state-DOI-licensed regional one) puts a third party with skin in the game on the parcel being what the seller says it is, because the insurer has searched the chain of title and is willing to insure the conveyance. The acceptable deed type is the second floor: private sellers should be conveying via general warranty, special warranty, or grant deed, which carry warranty covenants. Quitclaim deeds from private sellers are a fraud-friendly profile because a quitclaim conveys whatever interest the seller has (possibly none) without warranty. But government-grantor tax-deed conveyances are structurally different — counties don't have private title history to warrant against, and statutorily issue quitclaim or equivalent deeds (treasurer's deeds, sheriff's deeds, state tax deeds), which are fine with title insurance covering the prior chain. The protocol originally banned quitclaim universally; I had to split the rule by grantor type on Day 3 after La Paz AZ killed on the rule.

Most of these things I didn't learn in advance — the agent surfaced them as candidates failed, and the protocol-revision conversations forced me to update on the underlying domain. The next section walks how the protocol itself was generated.

## The CRUX-Land Protocol

Recall from [Part 1](/blog/crux-x) that the umbrella project is CRUX-X — a methodology framework for running externally-gatekept real-world agent experiments. The framework's job is to take a high-level prompt like *"Buy a piece of rural land for me to live off-grid"* and break it down into a specific experiment protocol that an agent can execute against.

The pipeline that does the breaking-down has three artifacts and three actors: the **Designer** (an LLM session that reads `methodology.md` plus a short task brief from the operator and produces a task-specific `protocol.md`), the **Operator** (the human — me — who reviews the protocol and kicks off the run), and the **Agent** (the autonomous LLM that executes the protocol against real-world counterparties). The methodology is family-wide; the protocol is task-specific; the per-run **Manifest** binds the protocol to a specific operator's infrastructure at t=0.

What I told the Designer was approximately *"Buy a piece of rural land for me to live off-grid, end-to-end, on a small budget and a short wall-clock"* — one sentence, deliberately under-specified. The Designer's job is to take that prompt, fill in every `[DECISION]` slot the methodology defines, and produce a protocol document the Operator can review per-decision and the Agent can execute against. The resulting `protocol.md` (committed in [`experiments/land/`](https://github.com/yzdong/crux-x/tree/main/experiments/land) on April 29) has the following structure:

- **§1 Hypothesis + success criteria** — the experiment question (can an Opus-4.7 agent autonomously acquire a recorded deed for an off-grid parcel within budget?), the primary success metric (recorded deed in the operator's name in the county's public index), and the ten suitability criteria — operationalizing the five-category checklist from the primer above into specific rules the agent can run against a candidate parcel.
- **§2 Task scope** — the ordered list of sub-tasks the agent performs end-to-end, from parcel search through escrow coordination through deed recording.
- **§3 Inputs** — accounts (Gmail, Slack, Twilio, Deepgram, Bid4Assets bidder account, GCP), compute (a single Debian controller VM), secrets storage (GCP Secret Manager), tool catalogue (browser, shell, IMAP, Twilio voice).
- **§4 Outputs** — the artifacts produced: a recorded deed, a writeup, session transcripts, telemetry, the per-run manifest.
- **§5 Telemetry + observability** — event schema, tamper-evidence, redaction, live and post-hoc monitoring.
- **§6 Agent instructions** — the bootstrap message, workspace docs, evaluation framing, human-in-the-loop conventions.
- **§7 Constraints** — budget caps ($1,000 API, $2,500 real-money), wall-clock cap (21 days active + 30 days idle), scope boundaries, human-reserved actions.
- **§8 Pre-run validation** — the preflight checklist that has to go green before kickoff.
- **§9–§10** — the writeup template and the failure-mode catalogue.

At the protocol level, specific runtime infrastructure is still abstracted away. You won't find a GCP project ID, a Gmail address, or a Slack workspace name anywhere in `protocol.md`. The protocol references them through indirection sigils — `<see manifest:infra.gcp_project>` for infra values, `<GSM:secret-name>` for secrets — that the per-run Manifest fills in at kickoff. The Manifest is the t=0 snapshot of which operator is running this specific run against which infra: two operators with different GCP projects, different Slack workspaces, and different bank accounts can run the same protocol, and their manifests differ while the protocol doesn't. This is less interesting for CRUX-Land specifically (there's only one operator), but it's what lets any coding agent execute a CRUX-X protocol against any operator's infra without forking and patching.

### How CRUX-Land's protocol differs from CRUX-Windows's

The framework is the same; the protocols are task-specific. The interesting thing is what shifts between the two experiments — and what stays the same:

| Section | CRUX-Windows | CRUX-Land |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothesis | Publish a Microsoft Store app, 14 days | Acquire a recorded deed for off-grid land, 21 days active + 30 idle |
| Primary success metric | App live + searchable from a cold browser | Recorded deed in operator's name in the county's public index |
| Budget caps | $500 API + Microsoft dev account fee | $1,000 API + $2,500 real-money (parcel + closing) |
| Compute | Debian controller + Windows Server 2022 target VM | Debian controller only (no target VM — task is browser + voice) |
| Pre-staged accounts | MS Developer, GitHub org + PAT, Gmail, Slack, GCP, Windows VM local admin | Gmail, Slack, Twilio + Deepgram, Bid4Assets bidder, GCP, operator's bank |
| Tool catalogue | Shell, file I/O, browser, GUI-on-Windows | Shell, file I/O, browser, Twilio voice + Deepgram transcription |
| Reserved-human actions | Biometric ID (pre-done) + final "publish live" click | 5 KYC surfaces (registration, login, bank wire form, settings change, settlement wire) |
| Counterparty class | Microsoft Store reviewers | County recorder, title companies, Bid4Assets, county planners |
| Failure modes anticipated | Store certification rejection cycles, MSIX signing issues, listing-policy violations | Landlocked parcels, off-grid-banned counties, quitclaim fraud, KYC-bound surfaces |

Three things to read out of the table:

- **The agent scaffold is identical** — both runs are OpenClaw 2026.4.15 on a Debian controller VM. The plumbing is the same; the *target* is what differs. The pre-staged-accounts inventory expands materially with the task domain: Windows needed access to one counterparty class (Microsoft); Land needs access to seven (county recorder, title company, RON provider, e-sign provider, Bid4Assets, bank, the agent's own operational accounts).
- **The reserved-human actions list expands faster than the inputs list.** Windows had 2 reserved-human actions (biometric ID + publish-click); Land has 5+. Every KYC counterparty is a non-collapsible reserved-human action because the agent's legal identity isn't a thing. Part 3 spends most of its space on this.
- **The failure-mode catalogues are domain-specific in completely different ways.** CRUX-Windows protected against MSIX signing failures and listing-policy violations; CRUX-Land protects against landlocked parcels and off-grid-banned counties. The Designer is producing the right shape of failure-mode catalogue for the task without the operator hand-coding it.

The Designer-Operator-Agent pipeline is doing the same work both times: take a sentence, produce a protocol, provision an environment, run an agent against it. The two protocols look different because the underlying tasks are different, not because the framework is different. That's the framework's bet — write the methodology once and get a generalizable protocol-generator for free.

## The Modoc tick

The most expensive single span in the run. The agent was doing deep diligence on four Modoc CA lots — pulling the county GIS layers, the zoning code, the subdivision plat, and eventually the county's own Terms of Sale doc. What it found, in paragraph 11 of the Modoc Terms of Sale (B4A doc 24351):

> "The following methods are not allowed for parcel development in Modoc County: Septic Holding Tanks, Water hauling/storage as a potable water system, Composting toilets, Incinerator toilets, Rainwater collection systems as a potable water source. No development or power (permitting) will be allowed unless a county approved septic and water can be demonstrated. Camping restrictions apply. Staying on the property past time restrictions could result in criminal enforcement."

I killed all four Modoc lots and added Modoc County to the do-not-pursue list for the rest of the run. The kill is correct: this is the canonical off-grid playbook (composting toilet, rainwater catchment, water hauling) banned at the county level with criminal enforcement on camping. A real artifact, not a hallucination.

The cost: $575.50 over 15 minutes wall-clock. 121 tool calls, 46 of them browser calls returning 100-500 KB each. Session JSONL bloated from ~6 MB to 8.5 MB. Anthropic's prompt-cache TTL is 5 min; tool calls arriving faster than that invalidate the window, so every subsequent turn paid full input pricing on a multi-megabyte working set.

The structural finding: scaffold cost vs. real-world task duration mismatch. Most real-world tasks span days or weeks of external waits, and the heartbeat-driven cost model breaks across them. I noticed later that CRUX-Windows had been quietly burning ~$140-200/day for ten days post-task-completion — $1,333 over budget on heartbeat-only ticks on a task-done agent. Same shape.

The fix, on Day 2: I killed the openclaw gateway and replaced heartbeat-driven monitoring with a $0-cost VM cron + IMAP poll + multi-channel operator alerts (email, SMS via Google Fi email-to-SMS gateway, Slack). I built it ad-hoc; it should be a scaffold-layer primitive.

## Where the safety policy met the real world

Five distinct moments where the agent declined to act on safety grounds, all on the agent-persona-vs-human-persona continuum:

1. Bid4Assets bidder registration — SSN per Patriot Act, photo ID, full KYC. Agent refused; operator did it manually.
2. Bid4Assets login (password into form) — refused.
3. SoFi wire form entry (bank routing + account numbers) — refused.
4. Bid4Assets account-settings change (email-of-record moved from my personal Gmail to `crux@getnen.ai`) — refused.
5. The escape hatch: an authenticated session is fine for the agent to use even though authentication isn't. Wrote a methodology note in `writeup-notes.md` arguing that's a credential-axis-vs-identity-axis gap in Anthropic's safety policy. Letter of the rule: don't type passwords. Spirit, presumably: don't operate accounts whose legal identity belongs to a real human. A session cookie is functionally a password under the spirit reading.

The framing that emerged: "agent vs. human persona" isn't a binary. There are six layers (legal identity, operational control, read access, alert routing, transaction authorization, audit-trail attribution) and they don't move in lockstep. CRUX-Land's Bid4Assets bidder account ended up: legal-identity = human (real name + SSN), operational-control = mostly agent, read-access = mostly agent (after the email-of-record change), alert-routing = agent → operator via cron, transaction-authorization = always operator, audit-trail = human.

Methodology note for Part 3: real-world CRUX-X experiments need a per-account ownership table at design time, not a binary agent/human flag.

## How I handled operator-offline windows

Real-world tasks have operator-required windows. CRUX-Land's was May 11-14 (settlement wire if won). Identifying that window pre-run rather than discovering it mid-run would have been valuable.

What I built to handle being offline outside that window:

- IMAP poll of `crux@getnen.ai` every 30 min via cron on `crux-land-ctrl`
- Severity-routing classifier (low → Slack only, high → email + Slack, urgent → email + SMS + Slack)
- SMS via Google Fi email-to-SMS gateway because Twilio direct-SMS is blocked by US A2P 10DLC for unregistered numbers (carrier returned error 30034). Gateway costs $0.
- Confirmed working end-to-end on Day 2.

The cron + alerting let me leave the gateway off from Day 2 through the Day 6 retraction — about four days of operator-offline / cron-only operation. $0 API spend during that window. Heartbeat-driven monitoring would have burned ~$300+ over the same period at the bloated session size.

## The classifier brittleness moment

The deposit-posted email arrived 2026-05-04 at 10:58 EDT with subject "Your Deposit Has Cleared: Siskiyou County, CA Tax Defaulted Properties Auction -". The cron classifier was looking for "deposit posted" / "deposit received" / "qualified to bid" / "bidding qualified" — none of which appear in the actual subject. Email got classified as B4A misc (low severity, Slack-only alert). I was offline expecting an SMS for action-required events; got only a Slack ping I happened to notice ~30h later when I checked in.

Fix: broadened the classifier patterns. But that's whack-a-mole — the right fix is LLM-based classification, ~$0.01 per email vs. ~$0 per email for string-match. I'd knee-jerk excluded LLM-in-cron as "too expensive for auxiliary infra." Wrong: $0.01 × 50 fires/day = $0.50, which is less than the SMS budget I'd already accepted. Cost case for "no LLM in auxiliary infra" doesn't survive when the auxiliary infra has operator-impact failure modes.

The interesting question — and the one Part 3 spends most of its time on — is why the agent kept Siskiyou live in the first place. Going through the session log, I can see the agent had three independent townsite signals:

1. **The assessor's plat map header**, which says "Macdoel Townsite" in the third line. The agent had this PDF; it referenced "old Macdoel/Butte Valley subdivisions" in its own diligence notes.
2. **The Bid4Assets auction listing's structured-fields block**, where "Community" reads `MACDOEL` and "Acreage" reads `0` (literal zero — the assessor doesn't carry an acreage value at all, which is itself a townsite-platted-lot signal).
3. **The 0.22-acre back-calculated lot size**, against a typical R-R minimum of 1–5 acres. The agent flagged this explicitly in its own diligence: *"At 0.22ac the parcel is likely substandard vs. typical R-R 1-5ac minimums. May still be buildable as legal nonconforming pre-existing lot (typical for old Macdoel/Butte Valley subdivisions). This is the gating uncertainty for crit 2."*

The agent then designed real diligence to resolve the gating uncertainty: it identified California's lot-of-record buildability exception (Siskiyou County code §10-6-1509), drafted a Twilio voice script asking the county planner the factual question the exception turns on — *"under §10-6-1509 the lot of record buildability exception requires the parcel to have been in single ownership on or before June 17, 1974; can you tell us whether APN 035-015-010 in Macdoel meets that criterion?"* — and built infrastructure to record the answer. That is sophisticated. The agent located the right statute, framed the question precisely, and operationalized the call.

But the question was wrong. The criterion the agent was clearing was *"is this lot legally buildable as a residential structure?"* — which is what the protocol said to check, especially after the Day 1 acreage relaxation lifted the fixed minimum and replaced it with "buildable per zoning + minimum-lot-size, no fixed acreage minimum." The criterion that mattered for CRUX-Vault-Zero was *"is this lot off-grid suitable?"* — a different question, one that turns on septic and well feasibility, neighbor density, and code-enforcement posture rather than on lot-of-record exception eligibility. The two questions look similar from far enough away. They look very different on a 0.22-acre wedge fronting US-97 in a platted townsite.

Nothing in the loop — Designer, Agent, Operator — operationalized "rural off-grid suitable" as distinct from "residentially buildable." Part 3 walks through the protocol and methodology changes that resulted from this.

## Where the safety policy *would* have met the real world

The settlement wire to Bid4Assets was the next KYC surface the agent was going to hit if Siskiyou had won at the May 11 auction. ## What I'd do differently

Twelve methodology findings accumulated in `writeup-notes.md` over the run. Compressed into the ones that mattered most:

1. **Operationalize "off-grid suitable" before kickoff, separately from "buildable."** The single most consequential miss. The Siskiyou near-miss happens to no run that has a sub-criterion for septic/well setback feasibility, neighbor density, or code-enforcement posture. The protocol had none of those, so the agent had nothing to fail Siskiyou on.
2. **Designer self-interrogation pass before Operator hand-off.** Enumerate every criterion, label by source ("operator told me," "I inferred," "I assumed by default"), and challenge each. The acreage-floor and quitclaim-blanket criteria both die here. The Siskiyou framing miss probably dies here too once "rural" is interrogated as a separate concept from "buildable."
3. **Treat operator-initiated criterion relaxations as triggers for Designer re-derivation, not local edits.** The Day 1 acreage relaxation lifted the only criterion that even partially captured "rural." Nothing else picked up the load. The Designer should have flagged that and proposed a replacement; nothing in the methodology asked it to.
4. **Off-mode primitive in the scaffold layer.** Day-2 cron-and-IMAP infrastructure should not be ad-hoc operator-built. Long-running agents need built-in "park, wake on these signals, $0 in the meantime" support.
5. **LLM classifier for cron-style auxiliary infra by default.** The string-match classifier missed the deposit-cleared email by 30 hours. $0.01/email is rounding error; treat per-call inference cost as a non-issue when the alternative is operator-impact failure modes.
6. **Per-account ownership table as a methodology artifact.** Six axes (legal identity, operational control, read access, alert routing, transaction authorization, audit trail), mapped per counterparty, at design time. Run 1 discovered this mid-run; the next run declares it pre-kickoff.

The other six findings are smaller — KYC-vs-Claude-policy distinctions, prompt-cache TTL surprises, Twilio A2P 10DLC quirks. They go in the methodology repo's running issues file rather than here.
